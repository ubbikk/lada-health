does ALEX 3 blood test  have  two-week steroid washout requirement or other reseaons for delaying?
No — ALEX³ does not have a standard “2-week steroid washout” requirement.
ALEX³ is a blood-based specific-IgE test, not a skin-prick test. Current ALEX³ guidance from the Military Institute of Medicine explicitly says that medications, including antihistamines and steroids, do not need to be stopped before testing. The NHS South Tees ALEX³ service similarly lists “no patient preparation required.” 
There is some lab-to-lab disagreement. For example, one Ukrainian laboratory advises against testing while taking glucocorticoids, especially systemic ones, whereas other ALEX laboratories explicitly allow them.  So if you've been given a specific “14 days” instruction, that looks more like that laboratory's conservative protocol than an inherent requirement of the ALEX³ assay.
The more relevant reasons to consider delaying are:
-  Recent major allergic reaction/anaphylaxis: the ALEX³ guidance above recommends waiting at least 4 weeks after an acute allergic reaction.  There is some scientific disagreement here: AAAAI says serum-specific IgE such as ImmunoCAP can generally be done immediately, although transient false negatives have been reported after certain types of anaphylaxis, particularly venom/drug reactions.  If the test is done soon after a severe reaction and comes back unexpectedly negative, repeating it later can therefore make sense. 
-  Long-term/high-dose systemic steroids: there isn't a defined ALEX³ washout period, but prolonged immunosuppression could potentially alter antibody levels. That's different from taking a brief steroid course and needing exactly 14 days afterward. 
-  Omalizumab/Xolair or another therapy that directly changes IgE biology: this can substantially alter IgE measurements and complicate interpretation. Omalizumab, in particular, lowers free IgE while increasing conventionally measured total IgE because of IgE–omalizumab complexes.  Ideally, an allergy profile is obtained before starting it when possible. 
-  Antihistamines: no washout is needed for ALEX³. They matter for skin tests, not serum specific-IgE testing.  
-  Fasting: not fundamentally necessary for the assay, although individual labs may request fasting or avoidance of fatty meals to reduce sample-quality problems.  
So, practically: if you took a short course of prednisone/dexamethasone/methylprednisolone and someone told you “wait two weeks,” I would not regard that as a universal ALEX³ requirement. I also would not stop prescribed steroids just to take the test.

Yes — and the discrepancy makes much more sense once you separate “the assay” from “the patient’s biology.”
ALEX³ is a blood test measuring allergen-specific IgE. The blood assay itself does not suddenly stop working after anaphylaxis. The question is whether the amount of allergen-specific IgE circulating in her blood can transiently change after a major reaction.
That is where the evidence becomes messy.
For skin testing, the phenomenon is reasonably established: after anaphylaxis, mast cells in the skin can temporarily become less responsive — sometimes called post-anaphylaxis mast-cell anergy. This is why skin-prick/intradermal tests are commonly postponed ~4–6 weeks. 
For blood specific-IgE, however, mast-cell responsiveness shouldn't matter because you're directly measuring antibody in serum. AAAAI therefore states that serum specific-IgE testing can be performed at any time after anaphylaxis. 
But there are scattered observations that serum specific-IgE itself can sometimes be temporarily lower shortly after anaphylaxis. A 2025 report, for example, describes cefaclor-specific IgE being negative immediately after anaphylaxis and becoming positive eight weeks later. The authors explicitly say further study is needed because we don't know how common this is.  Older reviews have also reported occasional reductions in specific IgE immediately after anaphylaxis. 
So we're dealing with:
-  Strong reason to delay skin testing. 
-  No convincing evidence that blood specific-IgE routinely becomes unreliable. 
-  A small amount of evidence that false-negative blood tests can occasionally happen. 
-  Almost no high-quality data telling us whether day 3, day 7, day 14, etc. is meaningfully different. 
That's why different institutions make different choices. One ALEX³ laboratory takes the conservative approach and explicitly recommends waiting at least four weeks after an acute allergic reaction.  Other allergy specialists consider serum IgE sufficiently reliable to test immediately. This isn't because they're running fundamentally different ALEX tests; they're making different risk-management decisions in an area where the evidence is weak.
There's even a survey showing how divided allergists are. Among responding US allergy fellowship directors asked about testing within six weeks after food anaphylaxis, about 59% wouldn't delay either test, 26% would delay skin testing only, and 15% would delay both skin and blood IgE. The authors' conclusion was basically: there isn't enough evidence, so practice varies. 
For your girlfriend, at 3 days after anaphylaxis, I wouldn't think of this as “ALEX³ is prohibited now.” A more useful strategy is:
Do the ALEX³ now if getting information now is important — but treat a negative result for the suspected culprit as provisional.
If it shows strong specific IgE to something that fits exactly with what she was exposed to before the reaction, that's useful information. If it says she's negative to the thing you strongly suspect caused the anaphylaxis, I would not use that negative result to declare her safe. Repeat targeted specific-IgE/ALEX or otherwise investigate it with an allergist after roughly 4–6 weeks.
And there's an important asymmetry here: waiting four weeks costs you information now; testing now doesn't prevent you from repeating it. So if you're urgently trying to figure out what she needs to avoid, doing the blood test now can be quite reasonable as long as everyone understands the limitation.
One other thing I'd consider at day 3: a baseline serum tryptase. It's a different test from ALEX. The acute tryptase window from the actual reaction has unfortunately passed, but a baseline tryptase taken after recovery can still be useful after severe, unexplained, recurrent, or venom-associated anaphylaxis. EAACI specifically includes tryptase measurements in anaphylaxis evaluation. 
Also, ALEX³ detects sensitization, not necessarily “this was the thing that caused Tuesday's anaphylaxis.” The exposure history from the few hours before the reaction is extremely important.
