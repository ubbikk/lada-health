Yes. I think that structure is substantially better.

Right now the document is organized partly by “things we know” and partly by “possible tests.” But from her point of view, the useful unit is a goal:

“Here is the problem → what do we need to learn → what changes depending on the answer?”

That also makes the appointment harder to derail into “here are five blood tests, see you later.”

One correction to your premise: figuring out the specific allergen is a major goal, but not the only major reason for testing. Some tests answer very different questions. Specific IgE/skin testing help investigate the culprit; tryptase helps characterize anaphylaxis/mast-cell activation rather than identify the allergen; spirometry addresses the separate-but-important question of asthma. citeturn666330search0turn666330search1turn666330search2

I’d structure the questions roughly like this:

1. What are these severe reactions?

The first goal is to establish what she’s actually dealing with.

Questions:
- Do the described episodes fit anaphylaxis?
- Could the recurrence of breathing difficulty 4–6 hours later have been a biphasic reaction?
- Does having ~5+ severe episodes change what should be investigated?
- Does baseline tryptase make sense in her case?
- If another severe episode occurs, would acute tryptase be useful, and when should it be taken?

This is where tryptase belongs. It does not tell you “she is allergic to mango”; it answers a different question. Current anaphylaxis guidance specifically gives baseline tryptase a role in recurrent/severe anaphylaxis and acute tryptase can help document mast-cell activation during an event. citeturn666330search1turn666330search7

2. What is actually triggering them?

This should probably be the biggest diagnostic section.

Questions:
- How convincing is mango as the trigger given the very clear 2022 episode?
- How do we explain reactions after drinks where mango was not obvious?
- Which specific foods/ingredients are worth testing based on the history?
- Would mango-specific IgE and/or skin testing be useful?
- If those results are negative or ambiguous, what is the next sensible step?
- Are there plausible cofactors — alcohol, NSAIDs, exercise, infection, etc. — that might explain why a food sometimes causes a severe reaction and sometimes does not?

This is also where I’d explicitly avoid “please test mango, cashew, pistachio, peanut, 40 fruits…” The diagnostic sequence is normally history → targeted sensitization testing → further testing only when it resolves a real uncertainty. A positive IgE or skin test means sensitization; by itself it does not prove that eating the food causes clinical allergy. When uncertainty remains, supervised food challenge is the diagnostic reference standard, although whether it is appropriate in someone with this history is very much a specialist decision. citeturn666330search0turn666330search31

3. How can she prevent another severe reaction?

This is more useful than a generic “diet” section.

Questions:
- What does she actually need to avoid until the work-up is finished?
- Which foods can she continue eating normally instead of restricting them unnecessarily?
- Does she need to avoid cashew/pistachio or other nuts merely because mango is suspected?
- Should alcohol just be treated as a possible cofactor around suspect foods rather than as an allergen itself?
- Does she need an adrenaline auto-injector, and what does she need to know about using it?
- Is there anything about her particular history — including a possible biphasic reaction — that changes her emergency plan?

Alcohol fits nicely here as one small point. It can act as a cofactor in food anaphylaxis, meaning it may lower the threshold or increase severity, but that is quite different from concluding that “she is allergic to alcohol.” citeturn666330search19turn666330search29

4. What is going on with the seasonal breathing problems?

I’d give this its own section because it may be clinically important independently of the food reactions.

Questions:
- Are the August episodes of wheezing, chest tightness and breathlessness suggestive of asthma/bronchospasm rather than just rhinitis?
- Does she need spirometry with bronchodilator testing or other asthma testing?
- Is her current treatment appropriate, particularly given that she is sometimes using Pulmicort + Berodual?
- What should her regular treatment during ragweed season look like?
- Is confirming ragweed sensitization useful, and would Amb a 1 testing matter if immunotherapy is being considered?
- Is allergen immunotherapy worth discussing?

Objective demonstration of variable airflow limitation, commonly with spirometry/bronchodilator response, is central to establishing asthma rather than simply assuming that wheezing in August is “allergy.” citeturn666330search2turn666330search15

5. Are the seasonal problems related to the severe reactions?

I like this as an explicit small section because otherwise the two stories may get mentally blended together.

Questions:
- Are these likely two separate problems — seasonal respiratory allergy/asthma and food-triggered anaphylaxis — or is there evidence that they interact?
- Could poorly controlled asthma make a severe allergic reaction more dangerous?
- Is there any reason to think pollen sensitization is relevant to the suspected food trigger, or would that be overinterpreting it?

I would not frame this as “is ragweed causing the anaphylaxis?” There currently isn’t enough evidence for that. But asthma and other underlying conditions can affect anaphylaxis risk/severity, so figuring out her respiratory disease matters even if the trigger ultimately turns out to be completely unrelated to ragweed. citeturn666330search19

- Is the prolonged swelling/+3 kg after the last episode something that should be investigated separately?
